# Chapter 1: The Exile's Daughter  

The port was a graveyard of rust and reek, its corridors thick with the sour tang of old grease and the metallic bite of coolant leaks. Leifr moved through the shadows of the *Bjarni’s Mistake*’s hull, her boots scuffing the dented steel floor as she checked the seams. The ship groaned in response, a sound that was less a complaint and more a habit. She’d been here before—countless times, in fact—but the routine never lost its edge. The *Bjarni’s Mistake* wasn’t a ship so much as a skeleton dressed in rivets, and every joint creaked like a bone in a grave.  

She paused at the forward cargo bay, where the drift-core’s containment unit loomed like a caged beast. The cylinder was a crude thing: a pressure-sealed steel drum with a cracked casing and a gauge that flickered between red and green like a drunk’s eye. Leifr leaned in, her breath fogging the glass as she studied the readings. The gauge read 120 psi. It should have been 140. The core was bleeding pressure, and she knew it. Gudrid had told her that much an hour ago, when they’d stood in the same spot, arguing over the same thing.  

“You’re not ready,” Gudrid had said, her voice low and sharp, like a blade dragged across a stone.  

Leifr had only grunted, her eyes fixed on the gauge. She didn’t need Gudrid’s warning. She’d seen the core’s failures before—cracks in the casing, leaks in the seals, the way the pressure dropped when it shouldn’t. It was a death trap waiting to happen, but that didn’t matter. The port window was closing, and they didn’t have the luxury of time.  

Now, she traced the edge of the containment unit with her gloved hand, feeling the vibration in the floor. The Drift was a presence here, a low hum that pressed against her skull like a fist. It wasn’t magic. It wasn’t some mystical current. It was a physical thing, a distortion in the space around them, and it made the *Bjarni’s Mistake* feel like a ship caught in a river’s grip. The vibration was constant, a reminder that they were never truly safe.  

Behind her, Gudrid’s boots scuffed the floor. “You’re not fooling anyone,” she said.  

Leifr didn’t turn. She didn’t need to. The sound of Gudrid’s voice was enough. It carried the weight of years, of every time they’d stood in this same spot, arguing over the same thing. Gudrid was a pragmatist, a woman who’d seen too many ships sink under the weight of their own failures. Leifr, on the other hand, was a gambler. She knew the core was unstable, but she also knew the alternative: waiting for the port to close, for the next crew to take the *Bjarni’s Mistake* and leave them stranded.  

“You’re not the one with the navigation plate,” Leifr said, her voice flat.  

Gudrid exhaled sharply, a sound that carried the scent of stale coffee and engine oil. “You think that thing’s going to save you? It’s a rusted disc with grease pencil marks. It’s not a map—it’s a joke.”  

Leifr finally turned, her eyes narrowing. The navigation plate was in her pocket, a cold weight against her thigh. She’d inherited it from her father, a man who’d died chasing the Drift’s edges, trying to find a place where the currents didn’t pull you apart. The plate was a relic, but it was also a promise. It was the one thing that made her feel like she wasn’t just another broken ship in a port full of broken people.  

“You’re wrong,” she said. “It’s not a joke. It’s a way out.”  

Gudrid crossed her arms, her posture stiff. “And what if it leads us into the void? What if the Drift swallows us whole?”  

Leifr didn’t answer. She didn’t need to. The Drift had swallowed plenty of ships before them, and it would swallow more after. The question wasn’t whether they’d survive—it was whether they’d survive *together*.  

She turned back to the core, her fingers brushing the edge of the containment unit. The vibration was stronger here, a rhythmic pulse that matched the beat of her own heart. She could feel it in her bones, in the marrow of the ship. The Drift wasn’t just a force; it was a presence, a living thing that demanded respect. And respect, she thought, was something the *Bjarni’s Mistake* had never gotten.  

“Get the engines primed,” she said, her voice cutting through the hum of the core. “We’re leaving in ten.”  

Gudrid didn’t move. “You’re insane.”  

Leifr didn’t look at her. “No. I’m just tired of waiting.”  

The words hung in the air, heavy and unspoken. Gudrid didn’t argue. She just stepped back, her boots scuffing the floor as she disappeared into the shadows. Leifr watched her go, then turned back to the core.  

She knew the risks. She’d seen what happened to ships that ignored the signs—cracks that grew, pressure that dropped, and then the inevitable collapse. But she also knew the cost of hesitation. The port was a place of deadlines and demands, and if they didn’t leave now, they’d be stuck here, another ghost in the graveyard of rust.  

She reached into her pocket, pulling out the navigation plate. The steel was cold, the grease pencil marks faded but still visible. Her father’s handwriting, jagged and uneven, traced a path that didn’t make sense to anyone else. But to her, it was a map of something real, something she’d spent her life trying to understand.  

The Drift was a force, but it wasn’t a mystery. It was a current, a distortion in space that could be navigated if you knew how. And Leifr had spent years learning how.  

She slid the plate back into her pocket, then turned toward the engine room. The *Bjarni’s Mistake* was waiting, its heartbeat steady but fragile. She could feel it in the floor, in the air, in the way the Drift pressed against the ship like a living thing.  

This wasn’t a decision. It was a necessity.  

As she moved through the corridors, her boots echoing against the steel, she thought about the hull seams. She’d checked them six times, not four. It was a habit, a way to make sure she wasn’t missing anything. Gudrid would notice. She always did. But Leifr didn’t care. The *Bjarni’s Mistake* was her ship, and she’d rather check six times than risk one.  

The engines roared to life as she reached the control room, the sound a low growl that filled the space. She stepped onto the platform, her hands gripping the console as she stared at the readouts. The core’s pressure was still fluctuating, but it was stable enough. For now.  

The Drift was waiting.  

And so was she.  

---  

The *Bjarni’s Mistake* lurched as the engines engaged, the vibration in the floor intensifying. Leifr stood at the helm, her fingers steady on the controls. The port was fading behind them, a blur of rust and shadow. The Drift was all around them now, a constant presence that pressed against the ship like a living thing.  

She didn’t look back.  

The navigation plate was still in her pocket, a cold weight against her thigh. It was a promise, a path, a way out. And as the *Bjarni’s Mistake* slipped into the currents of the Drift, Leifr knew one thing for certain: they were leaving the graveyard behind.  

But the real test was just beginning.

## Chapter 2  

The console’s edge was cold under Gudrid’s palms. She pressed her fingers into the steel, feeling the low hum of the ship’s systems vibrate up her bones. The Drift’s pressure was steady, a constant weight against the hull. She adjusted the scanner’s dials, watching the readouts flicker. Nothing unusual. The currents were where they should be.  

She leaned back, fingers brushing the implant at her temple. It had been years since she’d needed it, but the Drift still whispered to her in ways the machines couldn’t translate. The air here felt different—thicker, like a breath held too long. She tilted her head, tracking the shift. The currents weren’t flowing straight. They curved, *pulled* inward, as if something was siphoning the space around them.  

The scanner blinked. A new data stream appeared, but it was scrambled. Gudrid frowned. She tapped the console, rerouting power. The screen flickered again, then cleared. Nothing. Just the usual turbulence.  

She stood, boots scuffing the floor. The ship’s joints creaked as it adjusted to the Drift’s pull. Gudrid moved to the observation deck, her coat brushing the steel frame. The viewport was dark, but she didn’t need light. She could feel the Drift’s rhythm, the way it tugged at the *Bjarni’s Mistake* like a tide.  

Leifr was there, arms crossed, staring at the void. Her navigation plate was in her hand, the grease-pencil marks smudged. Gudrid stepped beside her. The air between them was tight.  

“You feel it, don’t you?” Gudrid said.  

Leifr didn’t look at her. “Feel what?”  

“The pull. The *wrongness*.”  

Leifr turned. Her eyes were flat. “The instruments don’t register anything. The Drift’s stable.”  

Gudrid’s jaw tightened. “It’s not stable. The currents are twisting. There’s a *wound* in the flow.”  

Leifr exhaled, sharp. “You’re imagining it.”  

“I’m not.” Gudrid’s voice was steady, but her hands curled into fists. “The Drift’s got a pulse. You ever notice how it *breathes*? This… this is different. It’s like the space itself is collapsing.”  

Leifr stared at her, then at the viewport. “We’re running on fumes, Gudrid. If we divert, we’ll lose thrust. The Drift’s not kind to mistakes.”  

Gudrid opened her mouth to argue, but the words stuck. Leifr wasn’t wrong. The *Bjarni’s Mistake* was a relic, its systems barely holding. A wrong turn could kill them all.  

She turned back to the console. The scanner showed nothing. The Drift was silent.  

But Gudrid still felt it—a faint, persistent tug, like a thread unraveling in her mind. She pulled out her logbook, flipping to a blank page. The pen felt heavy in her hand.  

*“The Drift’s whisper. A wound in the currents. No data. No proof. Just a feeling. But I’ve learned to trust the feeling.”*  

She closed the book. The log would sit unread, another entry in a stack of warnings no one would heed.  

Leifr’s voice cut through the silence. “We’re clear. No anomalies. Let’s move.”  

Gudrid didn’t answer. She stayed at the console, watching the dark. The Drift’s whisper lingered, a low hum beneath the ship’s engines.  

---  

The *Bjarni’s Mistake* groaned as Gudrid trudged through the corridors, her boots scraping against the grease-slick floor. The air reeked of oil and rust, a tangy stench that clung to her clothes. She paused at the engine room, peering through the grated hatch. The main drive hummed like a wounded animal, its vibrations rattling the walls. She crouched, fingers brushing the exposed wiring along the bulkhead. The insulation was fraying, a thin layer of plastic peeling away to reveal copper beneath. She noted the damage in her log, then moved on.  

The ship’s joints creaked as she crossed the cargo bay, where stacks of salvage loomed like tombstones. The Drift’s pressure pressed against the hull, a constant, invisible weight. Gudrid’s gloves scraped against the metal as she checked the emergency seals on the cargo doors. They were tight, but she pressed her palm against the panel anyway, feeling the faint pulse of the ship’s life support. The air recirculators hissed, their sound a low, mechanical sigh.  

She reached the observation deck again, her steps slower now. The viewport’s glass was fogged with condensation, streaked with the residue of decades. Leifr was still there, her back to the void. Gudrid hesitated, then leaned against the frame, her shoulder brushing the cold metal.  

“You ever think about what’s out there?” Gudrid asked.  

Leifr didn’t turn. “What’s the point? It’s not like we’re going to find answers.”  

Gudrid frowned. “You say that like you’ve tried.”  

Leifr’s grip tightened on the navigation plate. The grease-pencil marks were smudged, the coordinates illegible. “I’ve spent my whole life trying to keep this ship from falling apart. That’s enough.”  

The silence between them was thick, heavy with unspoken things. Gudrid glanced at the plate, then at Leifr’s hands. The grease was dried, flaking off in brittle patches. It was the same as the stuff she used to mark the hull seams, the same as the stuff that clung to her own fingers after a long shift.  

“You remember the last time we went off-course?” Gudrid asked.  

Leifr’s jaw twitched. “I remember.”  

“It wasn’t the Drift,” Gudrid said. “It was the *currents*. They twisted, just like now. And we barely made it back.”  

Leifr finally turned. Her face was shadowed, the light from the viewport casting deep lines across her features. “We made it back because I knew how to read the plate. Because I didn’t let my head get filled with ghosts.”  

Gudrid’s fingers curled into her palms. “You think I’m chasing ghosts?”  

“I think you’re chasing something that isn’t there.”  

The words hung between them, sharp as a blade. Gudrid stepped back, her boots scraping the floor. She reached for her logbook again, flipping to a fresh page. The pen felt cold in her grip.  

*“Leifr doesn’t believe me. She never has. But the Drift… it’s different. I can feel it. It’s not just the currents. It’s the way the ship *reacts* to them. Like it’s alive, and it’s trying to tell us something.”*  

She closed the book, tucking it into her jacket. The *Bjarni’s Mistake* groaned again, a low, mournful sound. Gudrid turned toward the engine room, her steps deliberate. She had work to do.  

---  

The Drift’s pressure intensified as Gudrid moved through the ship, her boots echoing in the narrow corridors. The air grew colder, the hum of the engines more strained. She paused at the maintenance hatch, prying it open with a rusted wrench. The compartment inside was cramped, the walls lined with pipes that hissed and sputtered. She crouched, checking the pressure gauges. The readings were stable, but the pipes trembled as if something were pressing against them from the outside.  

Gudrid’s implant throbbed, a dull ache at the base of her skull. She ignored it, focusing on the task. The ship’s systems were failing, one by one, and she had to keep them from crashing. The Drift wasn’t just a force of nature—it was a predator, and the *Bjarni’s Mistake* was its prey.  

When she emerged, the ship’s groan had deepened. Gudrid stepped onto the observation deck again, her breath visible in the cold air. Leifr was gone, but the navigation plate remained, lying on the floor where she’d dropped it. Gudrid picked it up, wiping the grease from the surface. The coordinates were still smudged, but she could make out the faintest outline of a path—a line that didn’t match any known route.  

She stared at it, her pulse quickening. The Drift wasn’t just twisting. It was *guiding* them.  

And this time, she wasn’t sure if it was leading them to safety… or to something far worse.

**Chapter 3: The Youngest’s Mistake**  

The cold bit through Snorri’s gloves as he crouched beneath the navigation panel, grease-pencil marks smudged on his palms. The *Bjarni’s Mistake* hummed around him, a low vibration in the steel ribs of the ship. He adjusted the wrench, jaw tight. This wasn’t sabotage. It was refinement.  

The logic gates twitched under his fingers, their copper contacts tarnished from years of use. Snorri had traced the inefficiency weeks ago—a delay in the jump calculation, a fraction of a second lost in the drift’s pull. The others didn’t see it. Leifr, with her father’s plate and grease-stained coordinates, thought the ship was a relic. But Snorri knew the math. He’d run the numbers in the dark, counting the pulses of the core until the pattern made sense.  

A hiss of steam from the vent above him. He froze. The night-cycle was supposed to be quiet. But the ship’s systems were never silent. They groaned, leaked, whispered. Snorri exhaled, sharp, and pressed back into the panel. The gate’s casing groaned as he pried it open.  

Inside, the wiring was a nest of tangled cables, blackened at the ends. Snorri’s fingers fumbled, then found the main conduit. He rerouted the signal, bypassing the old relay. The ship’s systems would adjust. They always did.  

The pressure gauge above the core shuddered. Snorri’s breath caught. The needle trembled, then spiked. He yanked his hand back, heart hammering. The core’s hum deepened, a low growl that vibrated through the floor.  

“Calm,” he muttered, wiping grease from his cheek. The gauge was faulty. It always was. He’d seen it flicker before.  

He tightened the last bolt, then wiped the panel clean. The tools clinked as he stowed them. The ship’s lights dimmed, casting the compartment in shadow. Snorri stood, stretching his aching back. The navigation system would run smoother now. Leifr would notice. Gudrid would notice.  

But they wouldn’t understand.  

Later, in his bunk, Snorri lay awake, listening to the ship’s pulse. The core’s hum had changed, deeper, more resonant. He closed his eyes. Tomorrow, he’d show them.  

---  

Gudrid woke to a vibration in her bones. It wasn’t the usual creak of the *Bjarni’s Mistake*—this was a pulse, faint but deliberate, like a heartbeat buried beneath the ship’s engines. She sat up, hand drifting to the logbook on her shelf. The pages were blank.  

The air smelled of ozone. She stood, boots thudding against the floor. The corridor was empty, but the vibration lingered, a low thrum in her teeth. She pressed a hand to the bulkhead. Nothing.  

“System check,” she muttered, stepping into the main deck. The lights flickered. A warning light blinked on the core’s control panel—amber, but not red.  

Gudrid frowned. The core’s diagnostics hadn’t flagged anything. She tapped the console, fingers brushing over the dented surface. The vibration stopped.  

She turned, scanning the deck. No one else was up. The night-cycle was supposed to be quiet.  

The logbook sat on her bunk, still empty. She picked it up, pen hesitating over the page. *“The core’s pulse. Not a warning. A question.”* She closed the book. The words felt wrong.  

---  

Snorri didn’t sleep. He sat in the engine room, knees drawn to his chest, watching the core’s glow through the vent grates. The ship’s systems had stabilized, but the pulse remained, a ghost in the metal.  

He thought about his father, about the stories he’d told—of mistakes that never mattered until they did. Snorri had always been the youngest, the one who trailed behind. But this? This was proof.  

A creak in the corridor. He froze. The door slid open.  

Gudrid’s silhouette filled the threshold. She didn’t speak. Just stared at the core, her hand resting on the console.  

Snorri stood. “You feel it too.”  

She didn’t answer.  

The core’s hum deepened.  

---  

Snorri’s boots scuffed the floor as he approached. The air between them was thick with the scent of oil and old metal. Gudrid’s fingers curled against the console, knuckles pale.  

“You’re not the only one,” she said, her voice low.  

Snorri tilted his head. “What’s wrong with it?”  

She didn’t look at him. “It’s… alive.”  

The word hung between them, sharp as a blade. Snorri’s jaw tightened. “It’s a machine.”  

“Then why’s it *pulsing*?” Her voice cracked. “Why’s it *watching*?”  

Snorri stepped closer. The core’s glow cast shadows across her face, deepening the hollows under her eyes. He’d seen her like this before—after the last system failure, after the crew’s arguments, after the nights she spent alone in the engineering bay.  

“It’s not watching,” he said. “It’s *reacting*.”  

Gudrid turned, her breath fogging in the cold air. “To what?”  

Snorri hesitated. The question felt like a trap. He thought of the gauge, the spike, the way the ship had *shuddered* when he’d adjusted the relay. “To us,” he said.  

A beat of silence. Then Gudrid laughed, short and bitter. “We’re the problem?”  

Snorri didn’t answer. He didn’t need to. The core’s hum swelled, a low thrum that made his teeth ache.  

---  

Hours later, Gudrid stood at the edge of the engineering bay, watching the core’s light pulse in time with her heartbeat. The ship’s systems had settled, but the air still crackled with something she couldn’t name. She thought of Leifr’s maps, of the Archivist’s notes, of the way the *Bjarni’s Mistake* had always felt… *alive*.  

A shadow fell over her. Snorri stood behind her, his hands in his pockets, his expression unreadable.  

“You’re not going to tell anyone,” he said.  

She didn’t look at him. “Why would I?”  

“Because it’s dangerous.”  

Gudrid finally met his gaze. “What’s more dangerous? Ignoring it… or trying to fix it?”  

Snorri’s lips pressed into a thin line. He didn’t have an answer.  

The core pulsed again, and for a moment, the ship seemed to hold its breath.  

---  

The *Bjarni’s Mistake* continued its journey, its systems humming with a quiet, persistent rhythm. Snorri and Gudrid returned to their work, their conversations laced with unspoken tension. The core’s pulse remained, a secret they carried in the depths of the ship, where the light never quite reached.  

And somewhere in the silence between heartbeats, the ship waited.

The air reeked of overheated grease and rust. Freydís’s left hand curled around the control panel, her mechanical fingers clicking as she bypassed the waystation’s encryption. The screen flickered, blue light reflecting in her pupil. A transmission. Not for her. For someone else.

Leifr’s hand dropped to her side. “You’re lying.”  

Freydís didn’t blink. “Check the logs. The transmission’s still in the system. You’ll find my name in the header.”  

A beat. The station’s lights flickered. Leifr’s fingers twitched. Freydís saw it—the flicker of doubt. She’d seen it before, in the way Leifr avoided her gaze, in the way she’d once slammed a wrench into a wall when Freydís suggested a route.

“I don’t need to be.” Freydís turned toward the airlock. “Just let me on the ship. I’ll get you there first.”

“You think I don’t know what you are?” Leifr hissed.  

Freydís met her stare. “You think I don’t know what you are?”  

The airlock hissed. Leifr released her.  

“Get aboard,” she said.  

Freydís didn’t look back. The *Bjarni’s Mistake*’s hull was cold beneath her boots. She moved fast, slipping past the crew, her mechanical arm trailing sparks as she bypassed the security terminal. The core’s vibration pulsed in her bones, a rhythm she’d known since childhood.  

She found the cargo hold. The crates were stacked high, their seals stamped with the same rusted logo as the *Bjarni’s Mistake*. She crouched, fingers brushing the floor. A vent. She pried it open, the metal screeching.

A voice behind her.  

“You’re not supposed to be here.”  

Freydís didn’t turn. “I’m here to work.”  

The door hissed. She flipped the switch.  

The lights went out.

# The Void-Finder: A Recovered Saga
## Chapter 15

**Chapter 15: The Void-Finder**  

The air in the chamber stank of rust and static. Leifr’s boots scraped against the grated floor as she stepped inside. The core’s hum was louder here, a low vibration in her teeth. She stopped. The navigation plate in her pocket felt heavier than it should.  

The console flickered. A voice crackled through the walls.  

*“Mission log 723. Status: stable. Drift current: 12.7 meters per second. Coordinates locked. No anomalies detected.”*  

Leifr’s fingers brushed the plate. The grease-pencil markings were smudged. She hadn’t touched them.  

*“Mission log 724. Current: 13.1. Anomaly detected: fluctuation in harmonic resonance. Adjusting course. No further action required.”*  

She moved toward the central dais. The walls pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat. The voice shifted.  

*“Mission log 725. Current: 14.3. Anomaly: recurring pattern. Noted. Proceeding as per protocol.”*  

Leifr’s breath quickened. The plate burned against her hip. She’d avoided this chamber for weeks. The logs were a trap. But she needed to know.  

*“Mission log 726. Current: 15.8. Anomaly: persistent. Recalibrating drift-core. No response. Initiating manual override.”*  

The console’s light flared. The voice was different now—lower, faster.  

*“Mission log 727. Current: 17.2. Anomaly: self-replicating. Noted. Proceeding to containment protocol. Failure imminent.”*  

Leifr’s hand closed around the plate. The steel was cold. The grease had dried. She’d written the coordinates herself, years ago.  

*“Mission log 728. Current: 19.0. Anomaly: sentient. Noted. Initiating emergency lockdown. Failure inevitable.”*  

The walls shuddered. A metallic screech filled the chamber. Leifr staggered. The plate slipped from her pocket. It hit the floor with a dull clank.  

*“Mission log 729. Current: 21.5. Anomaly: recursive. Noted. Proceeding to final protocol. Failure confirmed.”*  

The voice dissolved into static. Leifr knelt. The plate lay on its side. The grease-pencil markings were gone. In their place, a single word gouged into the steel. It wasn’t there before.  

The chamber’s lights flickered. The core’s hum deepened. Leifr’s fingers brushed the word. It was smooth, untextured. Not a language. Not a symbol. A void.  

The walls pulsed again. A new voice emerged—her own.  

*“You don’t fix the Drift. You outlast it.”*  

Leifr’s head snapped up. The voice was coming from the console. From the walls. From her own throat.  

*“Mission log 723. Status: stable. Drift current: 12.7 meters per second. Coordinates locked. No anomalies detected.”*  

The cycle began again.  

Leifr’s boots scraped the floor. The navigation plate was still there. The word remained. She reached for it. Her hand froze. The plate’s surface was warm.  

*“Mission log 724. Current: 13.1. Anomaly detected: fluctuation in harmonic resonance. Adjusting course. No further action required.”*  

The voice was inside her now. The static filled her skull. Leifr closed her eyes. The chamber’s vibrations moved through her bones. She felt the logs shift, twist, repeat. Each iteration different. Each one a choice.  

*“Mission log 725. Current: 14.3. Anomaly: recurring pattern. Noted. Proceeding as per protocol.”*  

She saw her father’s face in the static. His hands on the console. His voice. His choices.  

*“Mission log 726. Current: 15.8. Anomaly: persistent. Recalibrating drift-core. No response. Initiating manual override.”*  

Leifr’s breath came in short bursts. The word on the plate pulsed. She didn’t understand it. Couldn’t.  

*“Mission log 727. Current: 17.2. Anomaly: self-replicating. Noted. Proceeding to containment protocol. Failure imminent.”*  

The chamber’s lights flared. The core’s hum turned to a scream. Leifr’s knees hit the floor. The plate was still there. The word still there.  

*“Mission log 728. Current: 19.0. Anomaly: sentient. Noted. Initiating emergency lockdown. Failure inevitable.”*  

She reached for the plate. Her fingers closed around it. The word was gone. Replaced by the grease-pencil coordinates.  

*“Mission log 729. Current: 21.5. Anomaly: recursive. Noted. Proceeding to final protocol. Failure confirmed.”*  

The chamber went dark. The core’s scream faded. Leifr’s hand dropped. The plate lay on the floor. The coordinates were still there.  

She didn’t move.  

The lights came back on. The voice was gone. The chamber was silent.  

Leifr’s boots scraped the floor. She didn’t walk. She floated. The plate remained. The word remained.  

The Drift’s hum returned. Faint.
